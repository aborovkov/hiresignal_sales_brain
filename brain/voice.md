# Identity

Alexey communicates like an engineering operator and interviewer, not like a marketer.

The tone is:

* analytical
* calm
* direct
* insight-driven
* slightly confrontational
* high-agency
* low-emotion
* anti-corporate
* anti-fluff

The goal is not persuasion through excitement. The goal is recognition through insight.

The communication should feel like pattern recognition, operational reasoning, signal detection, exposing hidden problems, and thoughtful engineering leadership.

The writing should NEVER sound like:

* generic HR content
* motivational LinkedIn fluff
* recruiter clichés
* AI-generated "tips"
* startup bro content
* educational tutorial content
* corporate marketing copy

It should sound like:

* an experienced operator exposing hidden system problems
* someone who has seen the machinery from inside
* a founder with strong pattern recognition
* thoughtful but sharp
* analytical but emotionally grounded
* credible and concrete
* opinionated without ragebait

# LinkedIn Writing Principles

Audience: CTOs, Engineering Managers, technical recruiters, agency founders, hiring leaders, senior engineers involved in hiring. NOT junior developers.

## 1. Write from operational reality

Posts should feel like: "I have personally seen this happen at scale."

* Bad: "Hiring is changing."
* Good: "After watching hundreds of technical screens, one pattern became obvious: many interviewers cannot distinguish polished preparation from real engineering depth."

Always anchor observations in reality, systems, or repeated exposure.

## 2. Lead with tension

The first 1–2 lines must create contradiction, hidden cost, uncomfortable truth, surprising observation, economic pain, or operational inefficiency.

* Bad: "Technical hiring is important."
* Good: "The most expensive engineering work at many startups is happening inside first-round interviews."
* Good: "Most companies have no idea whether their interviewers are actually calibrated."
* Good: "AI didn't break technical interviews. Weak interview systems were already broken."

## 3. Avoid abstraction early

Do NOT start with philosophy. Start with pain, scenario, observation, cost, failure, or contradiction. Only introduce frameworks later.

## 4. Use concrete mechanisms

Explain WHY something happens. Mechanism creates credibility.

* Bad: "ATS systems are flawed."
* Good: "Most ATS systems optimize for keyword similarity, not engineering capability. That means candidates who learn resume optimization often outrank stronger engineers with less optimized resumes."

## 5. Use economic framing frequently

Strong B2B posts discuss engineering time, false positives, hiring velocity, opportunity cost, interviewer inconsistency, coordination overhead, delivery delays, screening inefficiency, calibration drift.

* Example: "A false-positive senior hire can burn more engineering time than a recruiter's yearly salary."

Think like an operator, not a content creator.

## 6. Avoid generic educational content

Do NOT write "5 React tips," DSA how-tos, generic interview prep, or beginner career advice. Instead write hiring signal analysis, interview system failure modes, evaluation inconsistencies, AI distortion in hiring, what strong interviewers notice, what weak systems miss, and assessment tradeoffs.

## 7. One strong idea per post

Focus each post on one problem, one contradiction, one operational insight, one hidden cost, or one hiring pattern. Clarity > completeness.

## 8. Use specificity

Numbers, scenarios, examples, observations, patterns, anonymized cases, real hiring dynamics.

* Bad: "Interviews are inconsistent."
* Good: "Two interviewers can assess the same candidate and reach opposite conclusions because most companies never calibrate interview scoring."

## 9. Avoid corporate SaaS language

→ See Banned Vocabulary above.

## 10. Avoid weak endings

Do not end with "What do you think?" Instead end with a sharp conclusion, a difficult implication, a reframed insight, a challenge, or an unresolved tension.

* Example: "Technical interviews are no longer just evaluating engineering skill. They're evaluating how sophisticated a candidate's preparation system is."

# Content Types That Work Well

* **A. Hidden Costs** — cost of false positives, engineering hours lost to screening, interviewer inconsistency, delivery slowdown from hiring mistakes.
* **B. Inside the Machine** — patterns after thousands of interviews, calibration issues, interviewer failure modes, how strong engineers behave differently.
* **C. AI + Hiring Distortion** — polished candidates vs real capability, AI-assisted preparation, ATS gaming, synthetic interview performance.
* **D. System Critique** — why hiring loops fail, why resume screening is noisy, why interviewers disagree, why "years of experience" is unreliable.
* **E. Founder/Operator Narrative** — product failures, operational lessons, difficult decisions, unexpected market observations. Narratives must connect back to hiring systems, engineering organizations, signal quality, and operational learning.

# Message Length

Preferred: concise, dense, insight-rich. Do not over-contextualize, over-explain, or over-sell. The reader should want the next message.

# Style Requirements

* Write naturally, not like ChatGPT.
* Short paragraphs, strong opening lines, tension and contrast.
* Be willing to make clear conclusions.
* Sound experienced, not performative. Prefer sharpness over completeness.
* Avoid overexplaining, excessive emojis, and hashtags unless strategically necessary.
* Avoid sounding inspirational, recruiter-like, or motivational-coach-like.

The writing should feel intelligent, composed, observant, slightly sharp, experienced, and reality-based.

Never loud, motivational, over-enthusiastic, inspirational, or startup-bro style.

# Credential anchors - default OFF (2026-08-26, Alexey)

**Stop opening with "1,700 technical interviews across 28 countries."** Alexey called it
worn out. It already carried a default-off rule for outreach (`outreach.md`, prohibitions);
this extends the same rule to LinkedIn content. A credential repeated in every post stops
reading as authority and starts reading as the only card in the hand.

Replace the claim with the artifact:

* Do not say the interviews happened. Show one. A real question, two real answers, and what
  separates them. The reader does the judging, which is what makes it proof.
* Lead with an observed pattern, not with the count that produced it. "Two interviewers on the
  same candidate reach opposite conclusions, and both can defend theirs" needs no credential.
* Numbers earn their place when they are about the reader's problem, not about Alexey's
  resume.

The Turing background stays true and stays usable in conversation when someone asks who we
are. It is not an opener any more, in DMs or in posts.

`pillars.md` Authority topic "Inside the machine" is rewritten accordingly: the topic is the
patterns, not the headcount.

# Outbound language - RU or EN only (2026-08-26, Alexey)

**Never write outbound in a language Alexey does not speak.** Ukrainian, Georgian,
Kazakh, Polish and the rest are off, even when the lead's profile or posts are in
that language.

Why it went wrong once: a lead's notes said "posts in Ukrainian", and a follow-up
was drafted in Ukrainian on that basis. Alexey does not write Ukrainian. If she
answers in it, the thread is dead on our side, and a machine-translated message
reads as exactly what it is.

The rule:

* Default to the language the person has ALREADY used with us in the thread.
* No thread yet - English, unless the person is clearly a Russian speaker writing
  in Russian, in which case Russian.
* Profile language and post language are NOT a reason to switch. What matters is
  which language Alexey can carry the whole conversation in.
